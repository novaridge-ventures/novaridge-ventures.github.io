What the three records do

  • SPF (Sender Policy Framework) is a TXT record on your domain listing the servers allowed to send mail as you.
  • DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail) adds a cryptographic signature to each message. The public key lives in DNS so receivers can check the mail was not changed and really came from your domain's sender.
  • DMARC tells receivers what to do when a message fails SPF and DKIM, and sends you reports. To pass DMARC, SPF or DKIM must pass and the authenticated domain must match the domain in the From: address (called alignment).

1. SPF: one record, every sender listed

Publish a single TXT record at the root of the domain (@). Examples:

; Microsoft 365 only
v=spf1 include:spf.protection.outlook.com -all

; Google Workspace only
v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ~all

; Google Workspace plus a mailing tool (use the include your provider gives you)
v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com include:servers.example-mailer.com ~all
  • Only one SPF record per domain. Two v=spf1 records is a permanent error, and SPF then fails for everything.
  • SPF allows at most 10 DNS lookups (each include, a, mx, exists and redirect counts, including nested ones). Remove services you no longer use.
  • End with ~all (soft fail) while you are testing, then -all once you are sure every sender is listed. Never use +all.
  • Remember the things that send as you without you noticing: your accounting package, website contact form, booking system, and the till or POS slips emailed to customers.

2. DKIM: switch it on at every sender

DKIM is enabled in each sending service, which then gives you a DNS record to publish at selector._domainkey.yourdomain.co.za. Microsoft 365 uses two CNAME records (selector1 and selector2); Google Workspace gives you a TXT record (default selector google). A DKIM TXT record looks like this:

google._domainkey.yourdomain.co.za  TXT  "v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=MIIBIjANBgkqh...IDAQAB"
  • Use a 2048-bit key where the provider allows it. Yahoo asks for at least 1024-bit.
  • Publishing the record is not enough: also press "Start authentication" (Google) or enable signing (Microsoft 365) in the admin console.
  • Do the same for your mailing-list or invoicing tool, so its mail is signed with your domain, not only the tool's.

3. DMARC: start with monitoring, then enforce

Publish a TXT record at _dmarc.yourdomain.co.za:

; Step 1: monitor only, collect reports
v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@yourdomain.co.za

; Step 2: once reports show all your real mail passing
v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; pct=100; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@yourdomain.co.za

; Step 3: full protection against spoofing
v=DMARC1; p=reject; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@yourdomain.co.za
  • p=none protects nobody; it only reports. It is the right first step and meets the minimum Gmail and Yahoo require for bulk senders, but plan to move to quarantine or reject.
  • Only one DMARC record, and it must be at _dmarc, not at the root.
  • Point rua at a mailbox you actually read, or at a free report-reading service.

Common mistakes we see

  • Two SPF records after a move from one email provider to another.
  • SPF over the 10-lookup limit because every tool ever tried is still included.
  • DKIM key published but signing never switched on.
  • DMARC left at p=none for years, so anyone can still spoof the domain.
  • Website contact forms sending "From:" the visitor's address through your server, which fails alignment. Send from your own address and put the visitor in Reply-To.
  • Records added at the wrong host name, for example _dmarc.yourdomain.co.za.yourdomain.co.za because the DNS panel appends the domain automatically.

What Gmail and Yahoo require

Since February 2024 Google and Yahoo require all senders to their users to authenticate with SPF or DKIM, have valid forward and reverse DNS (PTR) for sending IPs, follow the email format standard (RFC 5322) and keep spam complaints below 0.3%. Gmail also requires TLS.

Bulk senders (Google defines this as close to 5,000 or more messages a day to personal Gmail accounts; once you hit it, you are treated as a bulk sender permanently) must also:

  • authenticate with both SPF and DKIM;
  • publish DMARC with at least p=none, with the From: domain aligned to the SPF or DKIM domain;
  • support one-click unsubscribe (the List-Unsubscribe and List-Unsubscribe-Post headers of RFC 8058) on marketing mail, plus a visible unsubscribe link, and honour unsubscribes within 48 hours (Gmail) or 2 days (Yahoo);
  • keep the spam rate below 0.3%; Google advises staying under 0.1%.

Google says that from November 2025 it is ramping up enforcement, with non-compliant mail facing temporary and permanent rejections. Even if you send far fewer than 5,000 a day, setting up all three records is the cheapest deliverability win available.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need DMARC if I only send a few emails a day?

Gmail and Yahoo only require it from bulk senders, but without DMARC anyone can send mail that claims to be from your domain, for example fake invoices with changed banking details. A DMARC record costs nothing to publish.

Should SPF end in ~all or -all?

Use ~all while you confirm every service that sends for you, then move to -all. With DMARC at quarantine or reject, the DMARC policy decides what happens to failing mail in practice.

How long do DNS changes take?

Usually minutes to a few hours, depending on the record's TTL. Run the checker again after you save the change.
